Covalent Bonding

Sharing of electrons between atoms to form molecules

What is Covalent Bonding?

Covalent bonding is the sharing of electron pairs between atoms. Unlike ionic bonding where electrons are transferred, in covalent bonding both atoms share responsibility for the electrons. Covalent bonds typically occur between nonmetallic elements.

How Covalent Bonds Form

Electron Sharing

Two atoms approach each other and share electron pairs. Each atom wants to achieve a stable electron configuration (full outer shell). By sharing electrons, both atoms benefit from having more electrons in their outer shells.

Single, Double, and Triple Bonds

Single bond (H-H): 1 shared pair of electrons
Double bond (O=O): 2 shared pairs of electrons
Triple bond (N≡N): 3 shared pairs of electrons

Example: Hydrogen Molecule (H₂)

Each hydrogen atom has 1 electron. They share 2 electrons (one pair), so each achieves the stable helium configuration with 2 electrons in the first shell.

Types of Covalent Compounds

Simple Molecular Compounds

Small molecules held together by strong covalent bonds. Weak forces between molecules (van der Waals forces).

  • Examples: H₂O, CO₂, NH₃, CH₄
  • Low melting and boiling points
  • Usually gases or liquids at room temperature
  • Do not conduct electricity

Giant Covalent Structures

Large 3D networks where many atoms are bonded together by covalent bonds. The whole structure is essentially one giant molecule.

  • Diamond: Carbon atoms bonded in tetrahedral arrangement
  • Silicon dioxide (SiO₂): Extended network of Si and O
  • Graphite: Carbon atoms in layers, can conduct electricity
  • High melting and boiling points
  • Very hard (except graphite which is soft)

Properties of Covalent Compounds

Simple Molecules

  • Low melting and boiling points
  • Poor electrical conductivity (except when ionized)
  • Soluble in polar solvents like water (depends on polarity)
  • Weak intermolecular forces (van der Waals)

Giant Covalent Structures

  • Very high melting and boiling points
  • Very hard (strong throughout structure)
  • Poor electrical conductivity (except graphite)
  • Insoluble in most solvents

Polar and Nonpolar Covalent Bonds

Nonpolar Covalent Bonds

Electrons are shared equally between atoms of similar electronegativity (e.g., H-H, C-C, O=O).

Polar Covalent Bonds

Electrons are shared unequally. The more electronegative atom attracts the electron pair more strongly (e.g., H-O in water, H-Cl).

Polar Molecules

Even though individual bonds might be polar, the overall molecule is only polar if the bond polarities don't cancel out (e.g., H₂O is polar, but CO₂ is nonpolar).

Common Covalent Compounds

Water (H₂O) - Polar molecule, essential for life
Carbon dioxide (CO₂) - Nonpolar, linear molecule
Ammonia (NH₃) - Polar, nitrogen trihydride
Methane (CH₄) - Nonpolar, tetrahedral
Oxygen (O₂) - Diatomic, nonpolar
Nitrogen (N₂) - Diatomic, strong triple bond

Key Points to Remember

  • Covalent bonding involves sharing of electron pairs
  • Occurs between nonmetallic elements
  • Can form single, double, or triple bonds
  • Simple molecules have low melting/boiling points
  • Giant covalent structures have very high melting/boiling points
  • Polar bonds form between atoms with different electronegativities
  • Van der Waals forces act between molecules in simple covalent compounds
