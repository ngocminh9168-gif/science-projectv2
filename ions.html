Ions and Ionic Compounds

Charged atoms and their compounds in ionic bonding

What are Ions?

Ions are charged atoms or groups of atoms. They form when atoms lose or gain electrons. A positive ion (cation) forms when electrons are lost. A negative ion (anion) forms when electrons are gained.

Predicting Ion Charges from Electron Shells

The Octet Rule

Atoms tend to react to achieve a full outer shell (8 electrons for most, or 2 for first shell). This is the most stable configuration.

Metal Ions (Cations)

Metals lose electrons from their outer shell to achieve the stable configuration of the previous noble gas.

Sodium (Na): 2,8,1 → loses 1e⁻ → Na⁺ (2,8) charge: +1
Magnesium (Mg): 2,8,2 → loses 2e⁻ → Mg²⁺ (2,8) charge: +2
Aluminum (Al): 2,8,3 → loses 3e⁻ → Al³⁺ (2,8) charge: +3
Potassium (K): 2,8,8,1 → loses 1e⁻ → K⁺ charge: +1

Nonmetal Ions (Anions)

Nonmetals gain electrons to achieve the stable configuration of the next noble gas.

Chlorine (Cl): 2,8,7 → gains 1e⁻ → Cl⁻ (2,8,8) charge: -1
Oxygen (O): 2,6 → gains 2e⁻ → O²⁻ (2,8) charge: -2
Nitrogen (N): 2,5 → gains 3e⁻ → N³⁻ (2,8) charge: -3
Fluorine (F): 2,7 → gains 1e⁻ → F⁻ (2,8) charge: -1

Common Monoatomic Ions

COMMON CATIONS:
H⁺, Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Rb⁺, Cs⁺ (Group 1, charge +1)
Be²⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ba²⁺ (Group 2, charge +2)
Al³⁺ (Group 13, charge +3)
Ag⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺ (Transition metals, variable charge)

COMMON ANIONS:
F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ (Group 17, charge -1)
O²⁻, S²⁻ (Group 16, charge -2)
N³⁻, P³⁻ (Group 15, charge -3)

Polyatomic Ions

Some ions consist of more than one atom bonded together. These are called polyatomic ions.

OH⁻ Hydroxide ion
NH₄⁺ Ammonium ion
NO₃⁻ Nitrate ion
CO₃²⁻ Carbonate ion
SO₄²⁻ Sulfate ion
PO₄³⁻ Phosphate ion
HCO₃⁻ Bicarbonate ion
HSO₄⁻ Hydrogen sulfate ion

Writing Ionic Formulas

The Criss-Cross Method

To write an ionic formula, swap the charges and reduce to lowest terms.

Examples:

Sodium chloride: Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl
Magnesium oxide: Mg²⁺ + O²⁻ → MgO
Aluminum chloride: Al³⁺ + Cl⁻ → AlCl₃
Calcium carbonate: Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CaCO₃
Ammonium sulfate: NH₄⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → (NH₄)₂SO₄

Tips for Writing Formulas

  • Balance charges - total positive must equal total negative
  • Use parentheses for polyatomic ions when there's more than one
  • Write the cation (positive) first, then the anion (negative)
  • Reduce subscripts to lowest whole number ratio

Properties of Ionic Compounds

  • High melting points (strong ionic bonding)
  • High boiling points
  • Soluble in polar solvents like water
  • Conduct electricity when melted or dissolved (ions are free to move)
  • Brittle (layers shift, breaking bonds)
  • Exist as solids at room temperature (except some very soluble ones)

Naming Ionic Compounds

Rule 1: Metal + Nonmetal

Name the metal first, then the nonmetal with "-ide" ending.

NaCl → Sodium chloride
MgO → Magnesium oxide
CaCl₂ → Calcium chloride

Rule 2: With Polyatomic Ions

Keep the polyatomic ion name.

NaNO₃ → Sodium nitrate
CaCO₃ → Calcium carbonate
(NH₄)₂SO₄ → Ammonium sulfate

Key Points to Remember

  • Cations form when atoms lose electrons (positive charge)
  • Anions form when atoms gain electrons (negative charge)
  • Charge can be predicted from electron shells (octet rule)
  • Ionic formulas must balance positive and negative charges
  • Polyatomic ions are covalently bonded groups with overall charge
  • Use the criss-cross method to write ionic formulas
