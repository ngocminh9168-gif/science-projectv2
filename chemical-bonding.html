Chemical Bonding

How atoms combine to form molecules and compounds

What is a Chemical Bond?

A chemical bond is an attraction between atoms or ions that allows them to stick together to form molecules and compounds. Bonds form because atoms are more stable when they have a full outer electron shell. By sharing, transferring, or accumulating electrons, atoms achieve this stable configuration.

Chemical bonds are the reason why matter can form complex structures, from simple molecules like water to complex organic compounds that make up living organisms.

Ionic Bonds

How Ionic Bonds Form

Ionic bonds form between atoms when one atom completely transfers one or more electrons to another atom. The atom that loses electrons becomes a positively charged ion (cation), and the atom that gains electrons becomes a negatively charged ion (anion). The opposite charges attract each other, holding the ions together.

Characteristics of Ionic Bonds

  • Usually form between metals and nonmetals
  • Result in the formation of ions with opposite charges
  • Are generally very strong and require a lot of energy to break
  • Create compounds that are usually solid at room temperature
  • Many ionic compounds dissolve in water

Examples

  • Sodium chloride (NaCl or table salt): formed between Na⁺ and Cl⁻
  • Magnesium oxide (MgO): formed between Mg²⁺ and O²⁻
  • Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃): formed between Ca²⁺ and CO₃²⁻

Covalent Bonds

How Covalent Bonds Form

Covalent bonds form when two atoms share electrons with each other. Both atoms benefit from sharing because they can complete their outer electron shell. The shared electrons are attracted to the nuclei of both atoms, holding them together.

Single, Double, and Triple Bonds

  • Single bond: Two atoms share one pair of electrons (e.g., H-H)
  • Double bond: Two atoms share two pairs of electrons (e.g., O=O)
  • Triple bond: Two atoms share three pairs of electrons (e.g., N≡N)

Characteristics of Covalent Bonds

  • Usually form between nonmetals
  • Result in the sharing of electrons between atoms
  • Can be as strong as ionic bonds or weaker
  • Form molecules rather than ionic compounds
  • Many covalent compounds are gases or liquids at room temperature

Examples

  • Hydrogen gas (H₂): sharing between two hydrogen atoms
  • Water (H₂O): oxygen shares electrons with two hydrogen atoms
  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂): carbon shares electrons with two oxygen atoms

Polar vs. Nonpolar Covalent Bonds

Nonpolar Covalent Bonds

In a nonpolar covalent bond, electrons are shared equally between atoms. This happens when the atoms have similar electronegativity (attraction for electrons). Example: the H-H bond in hydrogen gas.

Polar Covalent Bonds

In a polar covalent bond, electrons are shared unequally between atoms. The atom with higher electronegativity pulls the shared electrons more strongly toward itself. This creates areas of partial positive and negative charge. Example: the O-H bond in water, where oxygen pulls electrons more strongly than hydrogen.

Metallic Bonds

Metallic bonds form between metal atoms. In metals, valence electrons are delocalized in an "electron sea" that moves freely throughout the structure. This explains why metals conduct electricity and heat well, and why they are usually malleable and ductile.

Why Chemical Bonding Matters

  • Determines how atoms combine to form all matter around us
  • Affects the physical and chemical properties of compounds
  • Is essential to understanding chemical reactions
  • Explains why some substances are solid, liquid, or gas
  • Enables the formation of complex molecules like proteins and DNA
