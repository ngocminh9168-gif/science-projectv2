Isotopes

Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons

What are Isotopes?

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have different numbers of neutrons. Because they have the same number of protons, they have the same atomic number and the same chemical properties. However, they have different mass numbers because the number of neutrons differs.

For example, carbon has three common isotopes: carbon-12, carbon-13, and carbon-14. All have 6 protons (making them carbon), but they have 6, 7, and 8 neutrons respectively.

Key Definitions

Atomic Number

The number of protons in the nucleus. All atoms of an element have the same atomic number.

Mass Number

The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus. Different isotopes of the same element have different mass numbers.

Notation

Isotopes are written as: Element-Mass Number

Chlorine-35 (17 protons, 18 neutrons)
Chlorine-37 (17 protons, 20 neutrons)
Uranium-235 (92 protons, 143 neutrons)
Uranium-238 (92 protons, 146 neutrons)

Relative Atomic Mass

Most elements in nature are mixtures of isotopes. The relative atomic mass is the weighted average of the mass numbers of all isotopes, taking into account their natural abundance (percentage).

Example: Chlorine

Chlorine-35: 75% abundant, mass = 35
Chlorine-37: 25% abundant, mass = 37
Relative atomic mass = (35 × 0.75) + (37 × 0.25) = 26.25 + 9.25 = 35.5

This is why chlorine has a relative atomic mass of 35.5 on the periodic table, which seems odd because there are no "half neutrons"!

Calculating Relative Atomic Mass

Formula: Relative atomic mass = Σ(mass number × relative abundance)
The relative abundance is expressed as a fraction or percentage.

Practice Example

An element has two isotopes:
Isotope A: mass 20, abundance 80%
Isotope B: mass 22, abundance 20%

Relative atomic mass = (20 × 0.8) + (22 × 0.2)
= 16 + 4.4 = 20.4

Why Isotopes Matter

  • Same Chemical Properties: Same number of electrons means same bonding behavior
  • Different Physical Properties: Different mass affects density, boiling point, etc.
  • Radioactivity: Some isotopes are radioactive (unstable)
  • Dating: Carbon-14 dating uses a radioactive isotope for archaeology
  • Medicine: Radioactive isotopes used in medical imaging (PET scans)
  • Energy: Uranium-235 used in nuclear power

Common Isotopes

  • Carbon-12: Most common, stable (12 nucleons)
  • Carbon-14: Radioactive, used for dating organic materials (14 nucleons)
  • Chlorine-35: Most common isotope (75% abundance)
  • Chlorine-37: Second common isotope (25% abundance)
  • Uranium-235: Fissile, used in nuclear power
  • Uranium-238: Most common natural uranium isotope

Key Points to Remember

  • Isotopes have the same atomic number but different mass numbers
  • They have identical chemical properties but different physical properties
  • The relative atomic mass on the periodic table is an average based on natural abundance
  • Some isotopes are stable; others are radioactive
  • The number of neutrons is: mass number - atomic number
