Reactivity & Valence

Understanding how electron shells determine chemical behavior

What are Valence Electrons?

Valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost shell of an atom. These electrons determine how an atom bonds with other atoms and how reactive it is. Atoms tend to react to achieve a full outer shell (stable configuration).

Finding Valence Electrons

Hydrogen (H): 2,1 → 1 valence electron
Carbon (C): 2,4 → 4 valence electrons
Oxygen (O): 2,6 → 6 valence electrons
Sodium (Na): 2,8,1 → 1 valence electron
Chlorine (Cl): 2,8,7 → 7 valence electrons
Argon (Ar): 2,8,8 → 8 valence electrons (full shell)

The Octet Rule

Atoms are most stable when their outer shell is full. For most atoms, this means 8 electrons in the outer shell (octet). Hydrogen and helium need only 2 electrons to be stable (duet rule).

Noble Gases - Maximum Stability

Noble gases (Group 18) already have full outer shells, making them extremely unreactive.

Helium (He): 2 (full shell, very unreactive)
Neon (Ne): 2,8 (full shell, very unreactive)
Argon (Ar): 2,8,8 (full shell, very unreactive)
Krypton (Kr): 2,8,18,8 (full shell, very unreactive)

How Reactivity is Determined by Valence Electrons

Metals (Want to Lose Electrons)

Metals in Groups 1, 2, and 13 have 1, 2, or 3 valence electrons. It's easier for them to lose these few electrons than to gain many to reach the next noble gas configuration. They become cations.

Sodium (Na): 2,8,1 → loses 1e⁻ → Na⁺ (2,8) like Neon
Magnesium (Mg): 2,8,2 → loses 2e⁻ → Mg²⁺ (2,8) like Neon
Aluminum (Al): 2,8,3 → loses 3e⁻ → Al³⁺ (2,8) like Neon

Nonmetals (Want to Gain Electrons)

Nonmetals in Groups 15, 16, and 17 have 5, 6, or 7 valence electrons. It's easier for them to gain a few electrons than to lose many. They become anions.

Fluorine (F): 2,7 → gains 1e⁻ → F⁻ (2,8) like Neon
Oxygen (O): 2,6 → gains 2e⁻ → O²⁻ (2,8) like Neon
Nitrogen (N): 2,5 → gains 3e⁻ → N³⁻ (2,8) like Neon

Periodic Table Trends in Reactivity

Group 1 (Alkali Metals) - Decreasing Reactivity Down the Group

All have 1 valence electron, but as you go down, the electron is further from the nucleus and easier to lose.

Lithium (Li): Least reactive
Sodium (Na): More reactive
Potassium (K): Even more reactive
Cesium (Cs): Most reactive (most easily loses its electron)

Reactivity increases down Group 1 because the valence electron is farther away and held less tightly.

Group 17 (Halogens) - Increasing Reactivity Up the Group

All have 7 valence electrons and need to gain 1, but reactivity increases as you go up.

Fluorine (F): Most reactive (strongest pull on electrons)
Chlorine (Cl): Quite reactive
Bromine (Br): Less reactive
Iodine (I): Least reactive

Reactivity decreases down Group 17 because the pull on gained electrons becomes weaker as the nucleus is farther away.

Group 18 (Noble Gases) - No Reactivity

All have full outer shells (8 electrons, or 2 for helium), so they don't need to gain or lose electrons. They're completely unreactive.

Predicting Chemical Reactions

The Golden Rule

Atoms react to achieve stable electron configurations (usually a full outer shell).

Using Valence Electrons to Predict Reactions

Example 1: Will Sodium and Chlorine React?

Sodium (Na): 2,8,1 → wants to lose 1 electron
Chlorine (Cl): 2,8,7 → wants to gain 1 electron
Perfect match! They react to form NaCl

Example 2: Will Sodium and Sodium React?

Sodium + Sodium: both want to lose electrons
No transfer possible, so they don't form a compound

Example 3: Why is Argon Unreactive?

Argon (Ar): 2,8,8 → full outer shell already
No reason to gain or lose electrons
Remains unreactive

Reactivity and Bonding Type

Metals + Nonmetals → Ionic Bonding

Metal (loses electrons easily) + Nonmetal (gains electrons easily) = Electron transfer = Ionic compound

Nonmetal + Nonmetal → Covalent Bonding

Neither wants to lose electrons completely, so they share electrons = Covalent compound

Metal + Metal → Metallic Bonding

Metals combine with each other, with valence electrons delocalized throughout the structure

Valence and Group Number

Group 1: 1 valence electron (1 oxidation state: +1)
Group 2: 2 valence electrons (1 oxidation state: +2)
Group 13: 3 valence electrons (1 oxidation state: +3)
Group 14: 4 valence electrons (can share 4)
Group 15: 5 valence electrons (usually -3 charge)
Group 16: 6 valence electrons (usually -2 charge)
Group 17: 7 valence electrons (usually -1 charge)
Group 18: 8 valence electrons (unreactive)

For Groups 1-3 and 13-17, you can often predict the charge: Group number - 18 (for anions) or Group number (for cations).

Activities Series (Reactivity of Metals)

The reactivity series ranks metals by how easily they lose electrons. More reactive metals are higher in the series.

Most Reactive ↑
Potassium (K) (group 1, most easily loses 1e⁻)
Sodium (Na)
Calcium (Ca)
Magnesium (Mg)
Aluminum (Al)
Zinc (Zn)
Iron (Fe)
Copper (Cu)
Silver (Ag)
Gold (Au) (group 11, least easily loses electrons)
Least Reactive ↓

More reactive metals can displace less reactive metals from their compounds.

Key Points to Remember

  • Valence electrons are in the outermost shell
  • Atoms want full outer shells (octet rule)
  • Metals lose valence electrons (become cations)
  • Nonmetals gain electrons (become anions)
  • Noble gases have full shells and are unreactive
  • Reactivity trends follow periodic table patterns
  • Group number often relates to valence electrons
  • Chemical reactions involve achieving stable configurations
