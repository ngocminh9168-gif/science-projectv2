Relative Atomic Mass

Understanding mass units and calculating relative atomic mass

What is Relative Atomic Mass?

Relative atomic mass (Ar) is the average mass of atoms of an element, compared to 1/12th the mass of a carbon-12 atom. It takes into account the different isotopes of an element and their natural abundance (how common each is).

On the periodic table, the numbers under element symbols represent relative atomic mass. For example, chlorine has a relative atomic mass of 35.5, even though no individual chlorine atom has exactly 35.5 nucleons!

Atomic Mass Units (AMU)

Definition

An atomic mass unit (u or amu) is defined so that a carbon-12 atom has a mass of exactly 12 u. This is the standard reference point for measuring atomic masses.

1 u = 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom
Carbon-12 = exactly 12 u
Proton mass ≈ 1 u
Neutron mass ≈ 1 u
Electron mass ≈ 0.0005 u (negligible)

Why Carbon-12?

  • Carbon-12 is the most common isotope of carbon
  • Chosen as the standard because it's stable and consistent
  • Allows whole number mass numbers for most atoms

Mass Number vs. Relative Atomic Mass

Mass Number

The total number of protons and neutrons in a single atom (an integer).

Example: Chlorine-35
Mass number = 35 (exactly 17 protons + 18 neutrons)

Relative Atomic Mass

The weighted average of all isotopes based on their abundance (can be a decimal).

Example: Chlorine
Cl-35: 75% abundant
Cl-37: 25% abundant
Relative atomic mass = 35.5

Calculating Relative Atomic Mass

Formula

Relative atomic mass = Σ(mass number × relative abundance)

Where relative abundance is expressed as a decimal (divide percentage by 100).

Example 1: Chlorine

Chlorine-35: mass 35, abundance 75% (0.75)
Chlorine-37: mass 37, abundance 25% (0.25)

Ar = (35 × 0.75) + (37 × 0.25)
Ar = 26.25 + 9.25
Ar = 35.5

Example 2: Boron

Boron-10: mass 10, abundance 20%
Boron-11: mass 11, abundance 80%

Ar = (10 × 0.20) + (11 × 0.80)
Ar = 2 + 8.8
Ar = 10.8

Example 3: Neon

Neon-20: mass 20, abundance 90.5%
Neon-21: mass 21, abundance 0.3%
Neon-22: mass 22, abundance 9.2%

Ar = (20 × 0.905) + (21 × 0.003) + (22 × 0.092)
Ar = 18.1 + 0.063 + 2.024
Ar = 20.187 ≈ 20.2

Working Backwards: Finding Abundance

If You Know Relative Atomic Mass

Sometimes you're given the relative atomic mass and need to find the abundance of each isotope.

Example

Element has two isotopes:
Isotope A: mass 24, abundance unknown
Isotope B: mass 26, abundance unknown
Relative atomic mass = 25

Let x = abundance of isotope A (as a decimal)
Then (1-x) = abundance of isotope B

25 = (24 × x) + (26 × (1-x))
25 = 24x + 26 - 26x
25 = 26 - 2x
2x = 1
x = 0.5 (50%)

So: Isotope A = 50%, Isotope B = 50%

Uses of Relative Atomic Mass

  • Molar mass calculations: The molar mass (g/mol) equals the relative atomic/molecular mass
  • Stoichiometry: Balancing chemical equations and calculating amounts
  • Identifying elements: Mass spectrometry identifies isotopes
  • Periodic table: Shows weighted average for each element

Common Relative Atomic Masses

Hydrogen (H): 1
Carbon (C): 12
Nitrogen (N): 14
Oxygen (O): 16
Sodium (Na): 23
Magnesium (Mg): 24
Chlorine (Cl): 35.5
Potassium (K): 39
Calcium (Ca): 40
Iron (Fe): 56

Key Points to Remember

  • Relative atomic mass is the weighted average of all isotopes
  • Carbon-12 is the standard reference (exactly 12 u)
  • Relative atomic mass can be a decimal
  • Mass number is always a whole number
  • Formula: Ar = Σ(mass number × relative abundance)
  • Relative abundance must sum to 1.0 (or 100%)
  • Numbers on the periodic table show relative atomic mass
