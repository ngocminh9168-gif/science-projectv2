Metallic Bonding

Sea of electrons model explaining metal properties

What is Metallic Bonding?

Metallic bonding is the attraction between metal atoms and a sea of delocalized electrons. Metal atoms lose their valence electrons to form cations, which are held in place by the attraction to the freely moving electrons around them. This creates a flexible structure with unique properties.

The Sea of Electrons Model

Structure

In metals, valence electrons are not fixed to individual atoms. Instead, they move freely throughout the structure, forming a "sea" of electrons that surround the metal cations.

Example: Sodium Metal

Each sodium atom has configuration 2,8,1. In metallic bonding, each Na atom loses its valence electron, creating Na⁺ cations surrounded by a sea of delocalized electrons.

Why This Model Explains Metal Properties

Electrical Conductivity

  • Delocalized electrons are free to move throughout the structure
  • They can carry electrical current without breaking bonds
  • Metals are excellent electrical conductors

Thermal Conductivity

  • Free electrons can move and transfer kinetic energy
  • Heat energy is quickly distributed through the metal
  • Metals are excellent heat conductors

Malleability

  • Metal layers can slide past each other without breaking bonds
  • Electrons rearrange to maintain metallic bonding
  • Metals can be hammered into sheets

Ductility

  • Metal can be drawn into wires without breaking
  • Sea of electrons maintains bonding during stretching
  • Metals can be drawn into thin threads

Luster (Shininess)

Free electrons can absorb and re-emit light, giving metals their characteristic shiny appearance.

High Melting Points

Strong metallic bonding requires significant energy to break, resulting in high melting points for most metals.

Metallic Bonding vs. Other Bond Types

IONIC BONDING:
- Electrons transferred
- Fixed in lattice
- Brittle
- Solids conduct poorly

METALLIC BONDING:
- Electrons delocalized
- Free to move
- Malleable/ductile
- Solids conduct electricity

COVALENT BONDING:
- Electrons shared (localized)
- Fixed between atoms
- Varies (often brittle)
- Poor conductivity

Strength of Metallic Bonding

The strength of metallic bonding varies depending on:

  • Number of valence electrons: More valence electrons = stronger bonding
  • Charge on cation: Higher charge attracts electrons more strongly
  • Atomic radius: Smaller atoms can pack more closely

Examples of Metal Properties

  • Sodium: Soft, low melting point (1 valence electron)
  • Iron: Hard, high melting point (multiple unpaired electrons)
  • Copper: Excellent conductor, very malleable
  • Aluminum: Lightweight, good conductor

Common Metals and Applications

Copper - Electrical wiring, plumbing
Aluminum - Lightweight structures, foil
Iron - Steel, structural support
Silver - Jewelry, electronics
Gold - Jewelry, electronics
Zinc - Galvanizing, batteries

Key Points to Remember

  • Metallic bonding involves delocalized electrons
  • Metal atoms form cations with a sea of free electrons
  • Free electrons explain electrical and thermal conductivity
  • Malleability and ductility result from flexible metallic bonding
  • Metals have high melting points due to strong bonding
  • Properties vary based on number of valence electrons
  • Metallic bonding is stronger with smaller atoms and more valence electrons
