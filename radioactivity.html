Radioactivity

Understanding nuclear decay and unstable atoms

What is Radioactivity?

Radioactivity is the process by which an unstable atom releases energy by emitting radiation. Some atoms have nuclei that are unstable because they have too many neutrons, too many protons, or an unfavorable ratio between them. These unstable atoms will spontaneously emit particles or energy to become more stable, and this process is called radioactive decay.

Marie Curie and Pierre Curie discovered radioactivity in 1896 while studying uranium, work that led to the development of nuclear science and medicine.

Types of Radioactive Decay

Alpha Decay

In alpha decay, an unstable nucleus emits an alpha particle, which is a helium-4 nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons). This reduces the atomic number by 2 and the mass number by 4. Alpha particles are relatively heavy and have low penetrating power.

  • Example: Uranium-238 decaying to Thorium-234
  • Blocked by: Paper or skin

Beta Decay

In beta decay, a neutron converts into a proton, releasing an electron (beta particle) and an antineutrino. This increases the atomic number by 1 but the mass number stays the same. Beta particles are lighter than alpha particles and have more penetrating power.

  • Example: Carbon-14 decaying to Nitrogen-14
  • Blocked by: Aluminum foil or thin plastic

Gamma Decay

In gamma decay, a nucleus releases energy in the form of gamma rays (high-energy electromagnetic radiation), similar to X-rays. Gamma rays have no mass or charge and are very penetrating. They often occur alongside alpha or beta decay.

  • No change in atomic number or mass number
  • Blocked by: Lead or thick concrete

Other Types

  • Positron emission: A proton converts to a neutron, releasing a positron (positive electron)
  • Electron capture: The nucleus captures an orbital electron to convert a proton into a neutron

Isotopes and Mass Numbers

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. Some isotopes are stable, while others are radioactive. The ratio of neutrons to protons determines whether an isotope is stable:

  • Light elements are stable with approximately equal numbers of protons and neutrons
  • Heavier elements need more neutrons than protons to be stable
  • Very heavy elements (like uranium) tend to be radioactive regardless

Examples of isotopes:

  • Carbon-12 (stable): 6 protons, 6 neutrons
  • Carbon-14 (radioactive): 6 protons, 8 neutrons
  • Uranium-235 (radioactive): 92 protons, 143 neutrons
  • Uranium-238 (radioactive): 92 protons, 146 neutrons

Half-Life

The half-life is the amount of time it takes for half of a radioactive sample to decay. Every radioactive isotope has a characteristic half-life, which can range from fractions of a second to billions of years. Half-life helps us understand how long a radioactive substance remains dangerous or useful.

  • Carbon-14 half-life: 5,730 years (used for dating organic materials)
  • Uranium-235 half-life: 704 million years
  • Uranium-238 half-life: 4.5 billion years
  • Polonium-214 half-life: 164 microseconds

Applications of Radioactivity

Medicine

  • Treating cancer with radiation therapy
  • Medical imaging with radioactive tracers
  • Sterilizing medical equipment

Energy

  • Nuclear power plants generating electricity
  • Providing power for space missions

Archaeology and Dating

  • Carbon-14 dating for organic materials (up to 50,000 years old)
  • Potassium-40 dating for rocks and minerals

Industrial Uses

  • Detecting faults in materials
  • Measuring thickness of materials
  • Sterilizing food

Nuclear Reactions

Fission

Nuclear fission occurs when a heavy nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing enormous amounts of energy. This is what happens in nuclear power plants and atomic bombs. For example, when Uranium-235 absorbs a neutron, it becomes unstable and splits.

Fusion

Nuclear fusion occurs when two light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus, releasing tremendous energy. This is what powers the sun and hydrogen bombs. It requires extreme temperatures and pressures to occur.

Safety and Protection

Different types of radiation require different protection levels:

  • Alpha particles: Stopped by paper or skin
  • Beta particles: Stopped by aluminum foil or plastic
  • Gamma rays: Require thick lead or concrete shielding
