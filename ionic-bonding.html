Ionic Bonding

Transfer of electrons between atoms to form ions

What is Ionic Bonding?

Ionic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between positively charged ions (cations) and negatively charged ions (anions). It occurs when one atom transfers electrons to another atom, resulting in the formation of charged particles that are held together by opposite electrical charges.

How Ionic Bonds Form

Step 1: Electron Transfer

A metal atom (which has few valence electrons) loses electrons to become a positively charged cation. A nonmetal atom (which has many valence electrons) gains these electrons to become a negatively charged anion.

Example: Sodium Chloride (NaCl)

Sodium (Na): 2,8,1 → loses 1 electron → Na⁺ (2,8)
Chlorine (Cl): 2,8,7 → gains 1 electron → Cl⁻ (2,8,8)

Result: Na⁺ and Cl⁻ attract electrostatically

Step 2: Electrostatic Attraction

The oppositely charged ions attract each other through electrostatic forces. Many ions arrange in a regular pattern called an ionic lattice structure.

Cations and Anions

Cations (Positive Ions)

  • Formed by atoms losing electrons
  • Typically metals (alkali metals lose 1, alkaline earth metals lose 2)
  • Examples: Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺

Anions (Negative Ions)

  • Formed by atoms gaining electrons
  • Typically nonmetals
  • Examples: Cl⁻, O²⁻, N³⁻

Properties of Ionic Compounds

High Melting and Boiling Points

Strong electrostatic attractions between ions require large amounts of energy to break, resulting in high melting and boiling points.

Solubility

Most ionic compounds are soluble in water. Water molecules can surround and separate the ions, dissolving the compound.

Electrical Conductivity

  • Solid ionic compounds: Do not conduct electricity (ions fixed in place)
  • Molten ionic compounds: Conduct electricity (ions are mobile)
  • Aqueous solutions: Conduct electricity (ions are free to move)

Brittleness

When ionic compounds are struck, layers can shift. Like charges repel, causing the compound to shatter.

Ionic Lattice Structure

Ionic compounds form regular 3D arrangements called ionic lattices. Each ion is surrounded by oppositely charged ions, and the pattern repeats throughout the crystal.

Examples:

  • NaCl structure: Each Na⁺ surrounded by 6 Cl⁻, each Cl⁻ surrounded by 6 Na⁺
  • CaF₂ structure: Different arrangement due to charge differences

Common Ionic Compounds

Sodium chloride (NaCl) - Table salt
Calcium chloride (CaCl₂) - Drying agent
Magnesium oxide (MgO) - Refractory material
Potassium bromide (KBr) - Photography
Aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) - Corundum, gemstones
Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) - Caustic soda

Key Points to Remember

  • Ionic bonding involves electron transfer from metal to nonmetal
  • Cations are positively charged (lost electrons)
  • Anions are negatively charged (gained electrons)
  • Electrostatic attraction holds ions together
  • Ionic compounds have high melting points
  • They conduct electricity when molten or dissolved
  • Ions arrange in regular lattice structures
