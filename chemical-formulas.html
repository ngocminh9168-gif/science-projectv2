Chemical Formulas

Writing and understanding chemical formulas for compounds

What is a Chemical Formula?

A chemical formula is a symbolic representation of the atoms present in a compound. It shows the type and number of each atom in the simplest ratio. The subscript numbers (written below the line) indicate how many atoms of each element are present.

Example:

H₂O means: 2 hydrogen atoms + 1 oxygen atom
CO₂ means: 1 carbon atom + 2 oxygen atoms
Ca(OH)₂ means: 1 calcium + 2 oxygen + 2 hydrogen (in hydroxide group)

Types of Chemical Formulas

Empirical Formula

The simplest whole number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound. It shows the relative proportions but may not be the actual molecular composition.

Molecular Formula

The actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule of the compound. It's a multiple of the empirical formula.

Example: Glucose

Empirical formula: CH₂O
Molecular formula: C₆H₁₂O₆
(The molecular formula is 6 times the empirical formula)

Ionic Formula

Shows the simplest ratio of cations and anions in an ionic compound. Always shows the compound in lowest terms.

Writing Ionic Formulas

The Criss-Cross Method

Step 1: Write the cation and anion with their charges
Step 2: Swap the magnitude of the charges to become subscripts
Step 3: Reduce to lowest whole number ratio
Step 4: Use parentheses for polyatomic ions when needed

Examples:

Sodium chloride:
Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl
(Charges are equal, so 1:1 ratio)

Magnesium chloride:
Mg²⁺ + Cl⁻ → MgCl₂
(Mg²⁺ has charge 2, Cl⁻ has charge 1
So need 2 Cl⁻ for each Mg²⁺)

Calcium carbonate:
Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CaCO₃
(Charges are equal, so 1:1 ratio)

Ammonium sulfate:
NH₄⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → (NH₄)₂SO₄
(Need 2 ammonium ions for each sulfate)
(Parentheses group the polyatomic ion)

Rules for Writing Chemical Formulas

General Rules

  • Element symbols use correct capitalization (C not c, Ca not CA)
  • If there's only 1 atom, don't write the subscript "1"
  • Element symbols are written in standard order (usually alphabetical)
  • For ionic compounds, write cation first, then anion

Polyatomic Ion Rules

  • If more than one polyatomic ion is needed, use parentheses
  • Write the subscript outside the parentheses
  • Example: Al(OH)₃ means 1 Al³⁺ with 3 OH⁻ ions

Simplification Rule

  • Always reduce to the lowest whole number ratio
  • Example: N₂O₄ can be written as NO₂ (the empirical formula)

Determining Formulas from Ion Charges

Method 1: Using Charge Balance

Find the smallest whole number ratio where: (Number of cations × Cation charge) = (Number of anions × Anion charge)

Example: Aluminum Oxide

Al³⁺ and O²⁻
Try 1 Al and 1 O: (+3) + (-2) = +1 (not balanced)
Try 2 Al and 3 O: (2 × 3) + (3 × -2) = +6 - 6 = 0 (balanced!)
Formula: Al₂O₃

Example: Iron(III) Sulfate

Fe³⁺ and SO₄²⁻
Try 1 Fe and 1 SO₄: (+3) + (-2) = +1 (not balanced)
Try 2 Fe and 3 SO₄: (2 × 3) + (3 × -2) = +6 - 6 = 0 (balanced!)
Formula: Fe₂(SO₄)₃

Common Ionic Formulas to Know

NaCl - Sodium chloride
CaCO₃ - Calcium carbonate
MgO - Magnesium oxide
Al₂O₃ - Aluminum oxide
Fe₂O₃ - Iron(III) oxide
Cu²⁺ - Copper(II) ion
(NH₄)₂SO₄ - Ammonium sulfate
Ca(OH)₂ - Calcium hydroxide
KNO₃ - Potassium nitrate
Na₂CO₃ - Sodium carbonate

Reading Chemical Formulas

Breaking Down C₆H₁₂O₆

  • C₆: 6 carbon atoms
  • H₁₂: 12 hydrogen atoms
  • O₆: 6 oxygen atoms
  • Total: 24 atoms in one molecule

Breaking Down Ca(OH)₂

  • Ca: 1 calcium atom
  • (OH)₂: 2 hydroxide ions (OH⁻)
  • Within each OH: 1 oxygen + 1 hydrogen
  • Total: 1 Ca + 2 O + 2 H atoms

Key Points to Remember

  • Chemical formulas show the ratio of atoms in a compound
  • Subscripts show the number of each atom type
  • Empirical formula is the simplest ratio
  • Molecular formula is the actual number in one molecule
  • Use criss-cross method for ionic formulas
  • Polyatomic ions need parentheses when there's more than one
  • Always balance charges in ionic compounds
  • Reduce to lowest whole number ratio
